Nobody Connects to Google Alone

There's a common assumption that a hotel simply signs up with Google and its rates appear. In practice, hotel rates reach Google through a technical connection maintained by a connectivity provider — a system that continuously delivers rates, availability and booking links in the structure Google requires.

This is why OTAs dominate hotel listings. They operate exactly this kind of connection at scale. The property itself is rarely the one talking to Google directly — something is doing it on their behalf.

So the real question for a hotelier isn't whether to use a provider. It's which one, and what it costs you.

What the Provider Is Actually Responsible For

It helps to be concrete about what this connection has to do, because it's more than a one-time setup:

  • Deliver rates continuously — not once, but every time anything changes.
  • Reflect real availability — a room that just sold must stop being offered.
  • Maintain the correct structure — Google's requirements are specific and not optional.
  • Route bookings to a working page — a price with a broken booking path is worse than no listing.
  • Stay live — a feed that silently stops is invisible until you notice bookings dried up.

Why a Bad Connection Is Worse Than None

This deserves emphasis, because hoteliers often assume any integration is better than nothing.

Consider a feed that lags. A traveller sees an old rate on Google, clicks through, and finds a different price on your booking page. They don't conclude that the software is slow — they conclude that you quoted one price and charged another. That's a trust problem, not a technical one.

Or consider stale availability. A room sold an hour ago is still showing as bookable. Someone books it. Now you're refunding a guest and explaining a mistake that was never yours to make.

The connection either reflects reality continuously or it manufactures problems. There's no useful middle ground.
