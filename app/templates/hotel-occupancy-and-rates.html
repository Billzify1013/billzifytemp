High Occupancy, Low Revenue: Why Being Full Isn't Always Good

Occupancy is the number every hotelier watches, and the one that misleads most often. A property can run consistently full and still earn far less than it should. Here's how that happens, and how to spot it in your own hotel.

⚡ The Short Version

  • Occupancy measures how full you are, not how well you did.
  • Selling out early is a warning sign, not a win.
  • Very high occupancy at a fixed rate almost always means the rate was too low.
  • The useful question is whether each date filled at the right price.

Occupancy Answers the Wrong Question

Occupancy tells you what share of your rooms were sold. It's easy to measure, easy to compare, and satisfying to watch go up.

What it doesn't tell you is anything about price. You can fill every room by charging very little. You can also fill every room by charging exactly what the market would bear. Occupancy looks identical in both cases, while the second earned considerably more.

That's the trap: a metric that rewards filling rooms, regardless of whether filling them was profitable.

Selling Out Early Is a Warning

Here's a specific signal worth learning to read.

When a date sells out weeks ahead of arrival, it feels excellent — the demand was there and you captured it. But think about what actually happened: your rate was low enough that all your rooms went before most travellers had even started looking.

Everyone who searched after that point found you unavailable. Some of them would have paid more than the guests who booked early. You didn't just undercharge — you closed the date before the higher-paying demand arrived.

A date that sells out very early is telling you the price was wrong, in the most polite way possible.

The Fixed-Rate Version of This Problem

The trap gets worse when the rate never moves, because then it's not a one-off misjudgement — it's a permanent condition.

Consider a hotel running ninety-plus percent occupancy year-round at a single rate. That sounds like an excellent business. But sustained occupancy that high, at an unchanging price, is strong evidence the price is too low. A rate that's genuinely right for a peak period would leave you with a few unsold rooms on ordinary nights.

Constantly full at one price doesn't mean you found the perfect rate. It usually means you found a rate low enough to fill in every condition — including the conditions where you could have charged much more.

What to Look at Instead

Rather than tracking one number, ask date-level questions:

  • How early did this date sell out? Very early means underpriced.
  • How did the rate compare with the same date last year? Your own history is the benchmark that matters.
  • Which dates finished empty, and how close did they get before you noticed?
  • Did the busiest dates earn proportionally more, or just fill faster?

These questions can't be answered by a single percentage, which is exactly why the single percentage is so misleading.
