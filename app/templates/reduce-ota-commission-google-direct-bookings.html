How to Reduce OTA Commission Without Losing Bookings

OTA commission is usually a hotel's largest recurring cost — and unlike rent or salaries, it grows precisely when business is good. The instinct is to cut OTAs. That's the wrong move. Here's the approach that actually works.

⚡ The Short Version

  • Commission scales with success — the busier you are, the more it takes.
  • Delisting from OTAs is the wrong fix; you lose the demand along with the cost.
  • The right approach is shifting the mix — winning more of the same demand directly.
  • Google is where that shift is most achievable, because the traveller is already searching for you.

Why Commission Feels Different From Other Costs

Most business costs are fixed or semi-fixed. Rent doesn't change because you had a good month. Salaries don't rise because occupancy hit ninety percent.

Commission does. It's a percentage of your success, deducted automatically, forever. A great season means a great commission bill. That's what makes it feel different from every other line item — and why hoteliers think about it far more than its category would suggest.

The Mistake: Cutting OTAs

The obvious response is to delist. Stop paying commission by stopping the channel that charges it.

This almost always backfires, and the reason is worth stating plainly: OTAs aren't just a cost, they're a source of demand. They bring travellers who would never have found your property otherwise — people searching a city, comparing options, with no particular hotel in mind.

Cut them and you don't just remove the commission. You remove the bookings. Most hotels that try this end up back on the platforms within a season, having lost occupancy to learn a lesson.

The Actual Fix: Change the Mix, Not the Channels

The right goal isn't zero commission. It's a better ratio — the same volume of business with a larger share arriving directly.

Think about it as two separate questions. Where does demand come from? And who captures the booking once the traveller has decided?

OTAs are genuinely good at the first. Where hotels lose money unnecessarily is the second — when a traveller who has already decided on your property still books through a platform, because that was the only option in front of them at the moment they were ready.

Where the Easiest Wins Are

  • Travellers who already know your name — they search your hotel specifically, then book through whichever link appears. That booking shouldn't cost commission.
  • Repeat guests — someone who stayed before shouldn't be routed through a platform to come back.
  • Google Maps searchers — arriving in a city and searching nearby, minutes from booking.
  • Guests who visited your website — they found you directly and then left to book elsewhere because there was no way to complete it.

None of these require you to generate new demand. The traveller is already there, already interested. The only question is whether booking directly is possible for them.

Why Google Is the Highest-Leverage Place to Start

Of all the ways to shift the mix, appearing on your own Google listing with your own rate and booking link is the most direct.

The reason is simple. When someone searches your hotel by name, or searches hotels in your area, Google shows a panel with booking options. If only OTA links are there, every one of those travellers books through a platform — including the ones who were specifically looking for you.

Adding your own link doesn't compete with your OTA channels for new demand. It captures the demand that was already yours, at the last step before the booking is made.

The Practical Path

Reducing commission comes down to three moves, in order:

  • Keep your OTAs — they bring discovery you can't replicate, and cutting them costs more than it saves.
  • Make direct booking possible — a booking engine on your own site, so a decided traveller can actually complete the booking with you.
  • Appear on Google with your own link — so that option exists at the exact moment travellers are choosing.
